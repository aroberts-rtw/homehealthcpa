A home health agency owner once described the experience this way: "My accountant tells me we had our best quarter ever. I just finished paying down the line of credit I had to draw on to make payroll for that same quarter." The confusion is legitimate. It reflects a real accounting phenomenon — not a mistake, and not an anomaly — and understanding it is one of the most practically useful things a home health operator can do.

## The core problem: revenue recognized before cash arrives

Home health agencies that bill Medicare and Medicaid recognize service revenue when care is delivered. Under accrual-basis accounting — which is what a properly maintained set of books uses — a visit completed on October 3rd appears as revenue on October 3rd. The claim gets submitted within a few days. Medicaid, depending on the state and payer type, typically remits payment 30 to 60 days after claim submission. Medicare Advantage plans can take longer.

That revenue sitting in accounts receivable is real — the claim is valid, the service was delivered, the payer will pay it. But the caregiver who delivered that service gets paid on Friday. Not in 45 days.

This is the structural source of what home health owners describe as the perpetual cash crunch: a profitable agency running at a tight cash position because the timing of expenses (weekly or bi-weekly payroll) doesn't match the timing of revenue collection (monthly or longer).

## How lines of credit actually function in this context

Most home health agencies with any meaningful volume carry a revolving line of credit. The mechanics are straightforward: the agency draws against the line to cover payroll when operating cash is insufficient, then repays the draw when Medicaid or other payer remittances arrive. The cycle repeats every month or every payroll period.

The line isn't a sign of financial distress in this context — it's a working capital tool. The credit facility is doing exactly what it's designed to do: bridging a timing gap between expenses incurred and revenue collected. A home health agency with a $500,000 annual payroll and a 45-day average collection cycle on Medicaid may need $60,000 to $80,000 in available credit just to manage normal operations without risk of a shortfall.

The problem arises when the books don't accurately reflect this structure. Two failure modes are common.

**Failure mode 1: LOC draws recorded as income.** Some agency owners, or their bookkeepers, record line-of-credit draws to an income or revenue account. This inflates revenue and distorts every ratio on the P&L. Gross margin appears better than it is. When the draws are eventually repaid, those repayments either appear as expenses (which deflates income in the repayment period) or are reconciled in a way that makes the books nearly impossible to read.

**Failure mode 2: LOC treated as a pass-through that doesn't touch the books.** Some agencies handle LOC draws and repayments entirely through a balance sheet account without running them through P&L, but also without correctly tracking the outstanding balance. The LOC liability on the balance sheet becomes stale, and the agency loses visibility into its actual leverage ratio and debt service obligations.

The correct treatment is not complicated: LOC draws are credits to cash funded by a corresponding increase in the LOC liability account. Payroll expense reduces cash. When remittances repay the line, cash increases and the LOC liability decreases. Interest expense is recorded separately as it accrues.

## What accrual vs. cash basis means for reading your results

Home health agencies have a tax election choice between cash-basis and accrual-basis accounting, and the choice has real implications for how monthly results appear.

On **cash basis**, revenue appears when cash is received. If October was your highest-volume service month and Medicaid remittances for October arrive in November, cash-basis accounting shows October as a weak month (little cash in, normal payroll out) and November as a strong month (high cash in, normal payroll out). Neither view accurately represents what the agency actually produced in that period.

On **accrual basis**, October's high volume appears as October revenue regardless of collection timing. October looks like October. The accounts receivable balance tells you what's been earned but not yet collected.

For management purposes — for any decision about whether to hire, expand territory, add a payer type, or take an owner distribution — accrual-basis reporting is almost always more informative for a home health agency. A P&L that moves in lock-step with Medicaid remittance cycles rather than service delivery cycles makes it difficult to evaluate actual operational performance.

This doesn't mean you must use accrual for taxes. Most small agencies elect cash basis for tax filing, which is permissible and often advantageous for tax timing purposes. But the internal management reports the owner uses to make decisions should be on accrual, even if tax returns are on cash.

## The PDGM overlay: how the payment model adds complexity

The Patient-Driven Groupings Model, which CMS implemented for Medicare home health beginning in January 2020, changed how the agency gets paid in ways that directly affect cash flow planning.

Under PDGM, Medicare payments come in two installments per 30-day period: a Request for Anticipated Payment (RAP) at the start of the period and a final claim payment at the end. The RAP advance is a percentage of the expected episode payment. When CMS reduced RAP percentages and then eliminated them for established agencies starting in 2021, agencies that had relied on RAP advances for working capital found their cash flow model disrupted.

For agencies with significant Medicare volume, the transition to full claims-based payment meant that a larger share of Medicare revenue is now collected at the back end of the service period rather than the front. If your bookkeeping doesn't separate Medicare from Medicaid, and doesn't track the sub-timing of Medicare payments under PDGM, you lose visibility into the component-level cash flow picture. A month that looks like a normal collection month may actually contain a cluster of delayed final claim payments from a high-volume prior period.

## Private pay and VA CCN: different timing, same structural issue

Private-pay clients — families paying directly for non-medical or companion care services — typically pay on a weekly or monthly invoice cycle. Collection timing varies by family and payment method. Families paying by check may run 10 to 20 days after invoice. Electronic payment or auto-pay arrangements can shorten this to a few days.

VA Community Care Network contracts have their own billing cycle and payment timeline. VA CCN typically pays 30 days from a clean claim submission through the TriWest or Optum (depending on region and contract year) network portal. The documentation standards for VA CCN billing are higher than most Medicaid programs, and claims that fail documentation requirements can age significantly before resolution.

All of this means that an agency with a mixed payer book — Medicare, Medicaid, private pay, and VA CCN — has at least four meaningfully different collection timelines running simultaneously. A chart of accounts that lumps all of these into "service revenue" makes it impossible to identify which payer class is contributing to a cash flow problem in a given month.

The solution at the bookkeeping level is a chart of accounts that separates revenue by payer class from day one: Medicare, Medicaid (further segmented by waiver program if relevant), private pay, VA CCN, and any other payer type the agency bills. The accounts receivable aging should be segmented the same way so the owner can see, at a glance, whether a cash shortfall is a Medicaid timing issue, a VA CCN documentation issue, or a private-pay collection issue.

## What the monthly close should show a home health owner

A monthly close for a home health agency should answer five questions without any manual digging:

1. **Revenue by payer class, accrual basis.** What did we earn this month, broken out by Medicare, Medicaid, private pay, and VA CCN?

2. **Collections by payer class.** What cash did we actually receive this month, and from which payer? This is the cash-basis view sitting alongside the accrual view.

3. **Outstanding accounts receivable by payer class and age.** What has been billed but not yet collected, and how old is it? Anything over 60 days in Medicaid deserves individual review.

4. **LOC balance and movement.** What was drawn, what was repaid, and what's the outstanding balance? This is the leverage indicator the bank watches and you should too.

5. **Cash-to-payroll ratio.** How many weeks of payroll does operating cash (not including LOC availability) cover? For most agencies, less than two weeks is a flag.

If your monthly close doesn't produce these five data points, you're managing based on a partial picture.

## Owner distributions and the timing trap

One of the most common planning errors in home health agency owner compensation: taking a distribution based on the P&L profit figure without accounting for the working capital float the LOC supports.

If the agency shows $40,000 in net income for the month on an accrual P&L but $25,000 of that represents revenue not yet collected (in accounts receivable), and the LOC balance is $30,000 higher than the prior month because of payroll funding, a $40,000 distribution would reduce working capital by $40,000 at a time when the agency actually needs additional cash to repay the line.

Distribution planning for a home health agency should be anchored to free cash flow — cash generated from operations after repaying any LOC draws made during the period — not to accrual net income. An owner who doesn't make this distinction will eventually find themselves in a situation where the P&L shows years of profitability and the balance sheet shows a growing line of credit balance.

The distribution safety calculation for a home health agency is: operating cash collected during the period, minus payroll and other operating cash payments, minus LOC repayments required to bring the line back to a target balance, minus a minimum operating cash reserve (typically two weeks of payroll). What remains is the discretionary distribution capacity. Not the accrual net income figure.

## A note on the cost-basis mechanics that most bookkeepers miss

Home health caregivers are compensated in ways that create bookkeeping complexity beyond simple payroll. Travel time between client visits, mileage reimbursements, and shift differentials for overnight or weekend care all need to be tracked accurately — both for correct expense recognition and for the labor-cost-per-visit calculations that determine whether specific service types (skilled nursing, personal care, companion care) are generating the margins the owner believes they are.

If caregiver payroll is tracked as a single expense line, you lose the ability to compare labor cost by service type. For an agency trying to decide whether to expand skilled nursing visits or focus growth on personal care, the per-visit cost structure by service type is decision-critical information that a generic P&L doesn't provide.

Building this visibility into the chart of accounts at setup — before three years of transactions have been recorded under a single "payroll" line — is substantially easier than reconstructing it later.

---

The cash flow mechanics described here are not edge cases for distressed agencies. They are standard operating conditions for any home health agency with meaningful Medicaid volume and weekly payroll obligations. Understanding them clearly — and having books that make them visible — is the foundation for every other financial decision a home health owner makes.
